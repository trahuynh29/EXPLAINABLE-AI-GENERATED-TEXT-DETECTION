Opening. Good morning. My report studies explainable detection of AI-generated text. The goal is a detector that does not just return a label, but writes out linguistic evidence first and then commits to a verdict. I will cover the motivation, the data and training pipeline, the results, which are mixed and which I will report candidly, and the next steps.

Motivation: why black-box detectors fall short

LLMs now write text that is nearly indistinguishable from human writing, and human judges perform only slightly above chance. AIGT is used for disinformation, phishing, review manipulation and academic misconduct.

Practical requirements now go beyond accuracy: under the EU AI Act, tools that monitor student behaviour during tests are classed as “high-risk” and must be transparent.

Three gaps in passive detection

  • Black box: supervised and zero-shot detectors return only a score or label, with no linguistic evidence (the “transparency gap”).
  • Threshold sensitivity and poor generalization: performance degrades on unseen domains, new generators and paraphrased text.
  • Post-hoc explanations (LIME, SHAP, saliency) have low faithfulness and can be manipulated.

Need: a passive detector that is accurate and generates its own linguistic evidence, which can be read, inspected and cross-checked against its decision. A false positive means wrongly accusing a human author.

Motivation. Passive detection is the realistic setting: we only see the final text. Existing detectors have three problems. First, they are opaque, and in an academic-integrity hearing an opaque score is not enough. Second, they are threshold-sensitive and generalize poorly across domains and to paraphrased text. Third, post-hoc explanations like SHAP or saliency maps are hard for non-experts to read and can be unfaithful. This points to one need: a detector that produces its own readable evidence. In education the cost of a false accusation is high, which is why this matters.

Reasoning-before-verdict framework

Instead of classifying first and explaining afterwards, the model produces a rationale c and then a verdict y that depends on it (following READER, Su et al., 2026):

fθ(x) → (c, y),   y ∈ {HUMAN, AI}
pθ(c, y | x) = pθ(c | x) · pθ(y | x, c)

A flawed chain entails a flawed verdict, and every verdict can be traced back to its evidence. The rationale (3–5 sentences, explicit connectives, quoting evidence from x) analyzes five forensic signals:

Style & Tone

Human: mixed registers. AI: neutral, formal, even.

Lexical Diversity

Human: repeats words, idioms. AI: even vocabulary, stock phrases.

Sentence Rhythm

Human: irregular short/long. AI: flat, even lengths.

Structural Symmetry

AI: repeated enumeration or transition templates.

Concrete Specificity

Human: experiential detail. AI: safe, generalized description.

Framework. The key difference from a classifier is not the number of outputs but the dependency between them: the verdict must be derived from the rationale. This is the factorization on the slide, and it is also what the SFT loss optimizes. The rationale is organized around five signals: style and tone, lexical diversity, sentence rhythm, structural symmetry and concrete specificity, each framed as a human-versus-machine contrast. One caveat from the report: the teacher prompt did not enumerate these five criteria, and the student prompt only names them, so they serve mainly as the analytical framework for judging rationale content.

Two-phase training pipeline and baseline

Proposed: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct

  • 4-bit QLoRA (NF4, double quantization), r = 16, α = 32, dropout 0.05, seven linear modules
  • SFT on 𝒟SFT (2,951): 2 epochs, lr 2×10−4, effective batch 16, max length 1,536
  • GRPO on 330 balanced samples (15 per label per domain) from 𝒟GRPO: 1 epoch, lr 5×10−6, group size 2, about 21 update steps
  • Output: JSON with rationale and verdict; greedy decoding; evaluation budget 160 tokens (200 during policy optimization)
R(oi) = Rformat + Racc,   Rformat ∈ {0, 0.5}, Racc ∈ {0, 1.0}
Ai = (ri − mean({r1…rG})) / std({r1…rG})

Baseline: RoBERTa-large

  • roberta-large, about 355 million parameters, direct binary classifier
  • Trained on 39,600 samples, 4,400 for validation (10%, stratified, seed 42)
  • 1 epoch = 2,475 steps, effective batch 16 (8×2), lr 1.5×10−5, cosine schedule, 250 warm-up steps, weight decay 0.01, 512 tokens, fp16
  • Validation: Accuracy 90.93%, Macro-F1 90.90%, AUROC 0.9806

Same 550 evaluation samples (25 AI + 25 Human × 11 domains) for both branches. The baseline outputs only a probability, so explanation metrics apply to the proposed branch only.

Training. The proposed model starts from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with 4-bit QLoRA. SFT teaches the output structure on the 2,951 filtered samples. GRPO then optimizes a two-part reward: 0.5 for a well-formed output and 1.0 for a correct verdict. These weights are my own design, since READER does not publish a formula. Due to dual T4 GPUs, GRPO ran only on 330 samples for about 21 steps, which turns out to matter. The evaluation generation budget was 160 tokens, below the 200 used during policy optimization. The baseline, RoBERTa-large, is trained on the same 44,000 samples, with 4,400 held out for validation, and evaluated on exactly the same 550 sample IDs.

Operating points and diagnosis of the two bottlenecks

Threshold 0.5 versus FPR = 1%

  • RoBERTa-large at 0.5: TPR 95.64% (263/275), FPR 13.82% (38/275).
  • At FPR = 1%: TPR falls to 67.64%; at FPR = 5%: 84.00%. Nearly a third of detection capability is lost in the low-false-alarm region.
  • Qwen labels only 7/275 Human texts as AI (2.55%) but detects only 20/275 AI texts (7.27%). It looks “safer” only because it rarely says AI.

Bottleneck 1: output truncation (FER = 40.73%)

  • None of the 224 failed responses ends with a closing brace (versus 100% of the 326 valid ones); 94.6% closed the rationale but were cut before completing the verdict key; only 8.00% reach it.
  • Failed responses average 839 characters versus 647 for valid; 47.3% reach 850+ characters versus 5.8%.
  • Input length correlates with format error: Spearman ρ = 0.321, p < 10−13, N = 550.

The 160-token budget is an evaluation-configuration failure, not a property of the model.

Bottleneck 2: bias toward Human

  • 𝒟SFT has 2,483 Human vs 468 AI (about 5.3:1), inherited from the teacher (Human recall 90.29%, AI recall 17.02%).
  • GRPO ran about 21 steps on 330 samples, too few to correct it. Result: AI-recall 7.27%.
Operating points and errors. At threshold 0.5 RoBERTa catches 95.64% of AI texts but falsely accuses 13.82% of human texts. If we demand only 1% false alarms, which is what an academic-integrity setting would want, TPR drops to 67.64%. At 5% it is 84.00%. For Qwen, a low false-positive rate is misleading: it labels only seven human texts as AI, but it also catches only 20 of 275 AI texts. It rarely accuses because it rarely concludes AI. I diagnosed two bottlenecks. First, truncation: failed outputs are longer and none closes the JSON object. The 160-token budget is below the 200 used in training, so this is an evaluation-configuration failure. Second, class bias: the SFT data is about 5.3 to 1 Human, inherited from the teacher, and 21 GRPO steps could not fix it. The two causes reinforce each other.

AI texts: surface repetition and formatting artifacts

Sample 134492 (ROC, gold AI, predicted AI). Input of 87 words contains an empty URL.

“. . . found this website, . This is a service provided by Energy.gov . . .”

The model flags the incomplete URL “.” (verifiable: “website, .” occurs in the input). It also cites “find information for use when finding” and calls it repeated, but the span appears only once: a matching citation can carry an unsupported claim.

Sample 221899 (SCI, gold AI, predicted AI). 53 words; an entire sentence repeats twice in a row.

The model notes that “Spatially coupled (SC) interleaving” appears twice and acknowledges the technical register “could also be generated by an AI designed to mimic such writing styles”. Verbatim match; real repetition cue; two-sided stance before concluding.

Human texts: colloquial phrasing and personal narrative

Sample 244008 (ELI5, gold Human, predicted Human). Argumentative reply.

“Slightly confused how you came to any of those conclusions. For the record, in the link provided . . .”

Correctly cites “For the record,” and “As far as your provided link goes,” but also asserts contractions “you’re” and “it’s”, neither of which occurs in the input: non-verifiable.

Sample 265204 (ROC, gold Human, predicted Human). 32-word short story.

“I needed to catch a train. I was on my way to work. Unfortunately when I got there the train left. I was devastated. . . .”

Cites “I was devastated” and notes a clear event sequence and personal reflection (matches), but the same rationale’s claim about contractions has no basis.

Two of the four examples (244008 and 265204) contain a citation absent from the text. The model learned the form of forensic reasoning (state a cue, quote a span, conclude) but does not guarantee every citation is real.

Case studies. Here are the four examples from the report. For AI texts, the model catches real surface artifacts. In sample 134492 it spots an empty URL, which does occur in the input, but it calls another phrase repeated when it appears once. In sample 221899 it identifies a sentence repeated twice, and keeps a balanced stance, admitting that formal technical register could also come from an AI. Summary of contributions and technical limitations

Three contributions

  • Standardized multi-domain dataset: 338,666-sample pool, balanced split (44,000 train / 9,600 test_9600), and a two-tier distillation set (𝒟SFT = 2,951; 𝒟GRPO = 41,049).
  • Robust baseline benchmark: RoBERTa-large reaches 90.91% Accuracy on 550 samples and 88.48% on the full 9,600; its bottleneck is REWRITE (79.71%).
  • Two-phase SFT + GRPO pipeline: the model learned the forensic reasoning style (90.0 words, 4.54 sentences, 95.71% / 76.07% surface proxies), though not yet baseline-level classification.

Three limitations

  • High output truncation: max_new_tokens = 160 with the verdict at the end of the output gives FER 40.73% (224/550); WP 68.0%, SQUAD 58.0%, SCI 54.0%. Truncated responses default to Human, pulling Accuracy to 32.00%.
  • Bias toward Human: GRPO on about 330 samples (about 21 steps) with 4-bit on T4 GPUs, and 𝒟SFT of 2,483 Human vs 468 AI. Human-F1 45.48% vs AI-F1 9.66%.
  • Computational cost and latency: a 7B model needs far more GPU memory and inference time than the 355M-parameter RoBERTa-large, a barrier for large-scale real-time moderation.

Also acknowledged: ±14 points per domain at n = 50; the 550-sample set is a subset of a 2,200-sample set used for pipeline checks; no human evaluation or masked-verdict probe.

Contributions and limits. The study delivers three things: a leakage-controlled multi-domain dataset with two-tier reasoning data, a strong baseline benchmark, and a complete SFT plus GRPO pipeline whose outputs show the forensic reasoning style. The limitations are equally clear. Truncation at 160 tokens caused a 40.73% format error rate. Human-class bias came from the teacher-filtered SFT data and a very small GRPO budget. And a 7B model is much more costly than an encoder. The honest conclusion is that the proposed model has not yet matched the baseline's classification performance, but the diagnosis is specific and the remedies are concrete.

Future directions

Token budget and constrained decoding

Raise max_new_tokens to 384–512 (re-run evaluation with 260–384 on the same 550 sample_id values) and apply constrained decoding / guided JSON so output always conforms, driving FER → 0%. Ablate a verdict-first structure.

pθ(c, y | x) = pθ(c | x) pθ(y | x, c)

Scaling RL and class balancing

Run GRPO on the full 41,049 samples of 𝒟GRPO on higher-capacity GPUs (e.g. A100-80GB). Rebalance 𝒟SFT by augmentation or reweighting, and add an anti-class-bias penalty to the reward. Penalize citations that do not match the input verbatim.

Speed and multilingual expansion

Distill the 7B model into lighter 1.5B–3B models to reduce latency. Expand the forensic reasoning dataset to Vietnamese for academic integrity and domestic content moderation, enabling cross-lingual evaluation.

Future work. Three directions follow directly from the diagnosis. First, fix the token budget, to 384 to 512 tokens, and use constrained or guided JSON decoding so the output always completes; I would also test a verdict-first ablation. Second, scale GRPO to the full 41,049 samples, rebalance the SFT data, add an anti-bias term to the reward, and penalize citations that are not verbatim in the input. Third, distill into 1.5B to 3B models to cut latency, and extend the dataset to Vietnamese. First step: re-run the evaluation on the same 550 samples with a larger budget, to separate format failure from classification ability.
